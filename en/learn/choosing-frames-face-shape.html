Choosing Frames That Suit Your Face Shape

Written by Alvin · Manh Long Optical, 147A Le Duan, Cua Nam, Hanoi · Updated 11 September 2026

In short: The classic rule is contrast: a frame shape should offset your face shape, not repeat it — angular frames soften a round face, rounded frames balance an angular one. Come to Manh Long Optical, 147A Le Duan, Cua Nam, to try a few shapes in person rather than guessing from a chart.

The principle that still holds is contrast: a frame should offset your face's lines, not repeat them. The rest comes down to width, weight, and whether the frame actually suits how you spend most of your week.

The contrast principle

Angular faces, square, rectangular, strong-jawed, soften under round or oval frames. Round, soft features read more defined in square or rectangular frames.

Oval faces genuinely suit most shapes, which says less about oval being flattering and more that, when no shape is ruled out, width and color become what actually decides it.

Width matters more than shape

A frame's widest point should roughly match your face's widest point. Noticeably wider or narrower is what actually throws off a photo, more often than the frame's shape itself.

Check three lines in a mirror: the top edge sits near your eyebrows, the lens doesn't touch your cheekbones, and the arms run straight back without pressing at the temples.

Acetate or titanium

Acetate reads warmer, holds a bold shape, and looks like a daily accessory. Titanium is lighter and finer, better suited to a pair worn from morning to late night.

Browline and cat-eye frames read more formal; round or square acetate reads more casual. Neither is objectively better, it's about which part of your week the glasses need to cover.

Just try them on

Photos lose the sense of fit. Ten minutes trying frames in person tells you more than an hour scrolling, and the shape that surprises you is often the one that actually works.

Once you've picked a frame, we adjust the nose pads and temple curve to your head, then measure your optical center height on that exact frame before cutting the lens.

Common questions

What frame shape suits a round face?
Angular shapes like rectangular or geometric frames add the definition a round face usually lacks.
Do glasses really change how a face looks?
Yes — a frame's width, shape and color all shift the visual balance of a face, which is why trying frames on in person matters more than checking a size chart.
Do thin-rimmed frames suit every face shape?
Mostly yes, since a thin rim adds less visual weight, and they suit a soft oval face particularly well.
Which frame color suits my skin tone?
Warmer skin tones usually suit tortoiseshell, gold or brown frames, while cooler tones suit black, silver or grey.
How do I read my own face shape at home?
Pull your hair back, look straight into a mirror, and compare the width of your forehead, cheekbones and jaw — the widest point tells you your basic shape.
How do I know what shape my face is?
Pull your hair back, look straight into a mirror, and compare three widths: forehead, cheekbones, jaw. Whichever is widest, and whether your jaw is angular or rounded, is enough to place your face shape.
Can a small face carry a large frame?
Yes, as long as it fits properly: the arm length is correct and the lens doesn't press against your cheekbones. A large frame only looks borrowed when it's wider than your actual face.
Does my prescription limit which frame I can choose?
Somewhat. Higher prescriptions look thinnest in a smaller, deeper frame, and a full-rim frame hides more of the lens edge thickness. Tell us your prescription before you fall for a shape that won't work with it.
