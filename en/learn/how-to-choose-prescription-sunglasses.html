How to Choose Prescription Sunglasses: Tint, UV and Frame Shape

Choosing prescription sunglasses comes down to four questions: how dark the lens should be, whether dark means protected from UV, which tint colour to pick, and whether your prescription suits the frame you like. This guide answers each one so you can decide before you pick a frame.

By Alvin Dao, Store ManagerPublished 27 Sep 2026Updated 27 Sep 20267 min read

Short answer

Pick the darkness for where you will wear them, not for how they look in the shop. Check that the lens filters UV, since a dark lens and a UV-blocking lens are not the same thing. Choose grey if you want colours to look normal, brown if you want a little more contrast. If your prescription is strong, a flatter frame usually gives clearer vision than a deeply curved sports wrap.

How dark should the lens be?

Darkness is measured by how much visible light gets through the lens. The European standard sorts sunglass lenses into five categories by that number. The scale is useful because a lens that feels right under the shop lights can be too dark on a grey afternoon, or not dark enough at midday.

If you ride or drive, the category 4 limit matters, because the standard marks those lenses as unsuitable for the road. For daytime use in the city, categories 2 and 3 cover the medium and strong sun conditions in the table.

At the counter we keep tint sample discs. Take one to the doorway and look at the street through it before you decide. Shop lighting flatters every tint.

Is a darker lens more protective?

No. Darkness and UV filtering are two separate properties of a lens. A dark tint cuts brightness. UV filtering blocks ultraviolet light, which you cannot see. A lens can be very dark and still let UV through.

Behind a dark lens your pupils open wider. If that lens does not block UV, the wider pupil lets more of it in, which is the opposite of what you want from sunglasses.

What to look for is a lens labelled UV400, which means it blocks light up to 400 nm and covers both UVA and UVB. The Wikipedia article on sunglasses gives the usual recommendation as blocking 99% or more of UVA and UVB. Every tinted lens we fit filters UV. A clear lens can filter UV too, which is how ZEISS UVProtect clear lenses work, so UV protection is not something only sunglasses give you.

For how UV protection differs from the blue-light filters sold for screen use, see blue-light filtering vs UV protection.

Grey, brown, green or gradient?

Tint colour changes how the world looks, not how well you are protected. Pick it for comfort and for what you do outdoors.

Depth of tint usually matters more for comfort than colour. A category 3 grey and a category 3 brown feel closer to each other than a category 2 and a category 3 of the same colour.

Does your prescription suit a curved frame?

Ask this before you fall for a frame. Many sunglass frames curve around the face. A prescription lens has to be ground into that curve, and the stronger the prescription, the harder it becomes to keep the edges of the lens sharp.

One US prescription safety lab gives these working limits for wrap frames with a base curve of 6 to 8:

  • Comfortable: about −4.00 to +3.00
  • Possible but difficult: up to about −6.00 to +4.00
  • Cylinder (astigmatism) above about 2.00 makes adapting to a wrap lens much harder

Those numbers come from one lab, so treat them as a guide rather than a hard rule. The practical point holds: with a strong prescription, a flatter frame with a moderate lens size usually gives clearer vision and thinner edges than a deep wrap. If your lenses are already thick, the lens index guide explains how a higher index keeps them slimmer.

At Manh Long Optical, short-sighted prescriptions up to about −5.00 work fine in a curved sunglass frame. If you are −5.00 or stronger, or also have astigmatism or long-sightedness, bring your prescription to the counter so we can check the specific frame before you decide. Checking first is quicker than changing frames after the lenses are cut.

Photochromic in Hanoi heat and in the car

Photochromic lenses are the other way to wear your prescription in the sun: one pair that is clear indoors and darkens outside. Two things about them surprise people.

First, heat. The chemistry that darkens the lens works less strongly when it is hot, so on a hot day the lens does not get as dark as it would in cool weather. It darkens within about a minute in sunlight, and it does not clear instantly when you walk inside; how long it takes depends on the brand and the temperature.

Second, cars. Photochromic lenses react to UV, and a laminated windscreen blocks most UV. Behind the windscreen the lens stays close to clear even in bright sun. If you drive a lot in daylight, a dedicated tinted pair works better.

For a side-by-side look at both lens types, read photochromic vs polarized lenses. For what the Essilor range costs, see Essilor Transitions prices.

Why not polarised with a prescription?

Polarised lenses block light reflected flat off wet roads and water, which is the glare that makes a road surface look white at midday. That is a real benefit for driving and for time near water.

They also have side effects worth knowing. Phone screens, car displays and other LCD screens can look dark or patchy through a polarised lens, and some car windows show coloured patterns.

At Manh Long Optical we sell polarised lenses without a prescription only. If you need correction and want to cut glare, the usual option here is a dark tint with anti-reflective coating on the back surface of the lens, which cuts the reflections that bounce off the inside of the lens into your eye.

Which setup fits you

  • If you spend long stretches outdoors and want proper sunglasses→A tinted prescription pair, category 2 or 3
  • If you go in and out of buildings all day and hate swapping glasses→Photochromic lenses
  • If you drive a lot in daylight→A tinted pair; photochromic stays light behind the windscreen
  • If your prescription is strong or has high astigmatism→A flatter frame rather than a deep wrap
  • If glare off water is the main problem→Non-prescription polarised over contact lenses, or a dark tint with back-surface anti-reflective coating

FAQ

Can I get sunglasses with a strong prescription?

Usually yes. At Manh Long Optical, short-sighted prescriptions up to about −5.00 work fine in a curved sunglass frame. Stronger prescriptions do better in flatter frames with a moderate lens size, and a higher-index lens keeps the edges thinner.

Which tint category works for riding a motorbike in the city?

Categories 2 and 3 cover medium to strong daytime sun. Category 4 is marked as not for road use in the European standard.

Do darker sunglasses block more UV?

No. UV filtering is separate from how dark the lens looks. Check for UV400 or a stated UV filter rather than judging by colour.

Will photochromic lenses replace sunglasses in Hanoi's summer?

Partly. They darken outdoors, but less in hot weather and hardly at all behind a car windscreen, so people who drive or spend long hours outside often keep a separate tinted pair.

One thing to remember

Choose the darkness for where you will wear the lens, check UV separately, and let your prescription decide how curved the frame can be.

A note on health: Sunglasses reduce brightness and, with a UV filter, block ultraviolet light. They do not treat any eye condition. If something about your vision worries you, see an eye doctor. Prices can change; ask in store before ordering.
