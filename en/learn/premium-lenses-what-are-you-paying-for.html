The three real cost layers in a premium lens

Lens design decides how many viewing distances the lens actually covers. Single-vision handles one distance, near or far. Progressive (multifocal) lenses combine several viewing zones on one lens surface. That takes a more complex optical design and tighter fitting accuracy, which is why progressives cost more than single-vision lenses — simply because the engineering is harder.

Coating is a surface treatment applied to the lens: anti-reflective coating cuts glare from screens and lights, scratch-resistant coating extends the lens's usable life, and blue-light filtering reduces part of the blue light coming from screens. On eye strain specifically, current evidence (a 2023 Cochrane review, the American Academy of Ophthalmology) hasn't shown a clear effect from blue-light filtering; the glare reduction from anti-reflective coating is what actually makes screen use more comfortable. Each coating is a separate processing step added on top of the base lens price.

Photochromic ability is a lens material that darkens outdoors and clears indoors on its own. It's useful if you move often between indoor and outdoor settings. M-LonG currently carries photochromic lenses that work with a prescription, not only as plano (non-prescription) sunglasses. If you need a pair specifically for strong sun glare, polarized sunglasses are still a separate, more effective option, currently only available at the shop without prescription power.

Underneath all three layers is the actual cutting and fitting work, getting the optical center and frame tilt right, which directly affects whether you get a few days of mild dizziness or not, especially with progressive lenses.

Which layer to upgrade, based on how you actually use your eyes

Consider blue-light filtering if you:

  • Spend most of your day looking at a computer or phone screen
  • Want less glare during long screen sessions

Consider photochromic if you:

  • Move often between indoor and outdoor settings during the day
  • Don't want to carry two pairs of glasses (prescription glasses plus sunglasses)

You probably don't need progressive lenses yet if you:

  • Only need one viewing distance corrected, near or far: single-vision already covers that
  • Haven't noticed trouble focusing up close as you get older (presbyopia): see our guide on how nearsighted you need to be for glasses

What to check before committing to an upgrade

1. Each layer should be its own line item

If a quote bundles everything into one "premium package" number, ask for it broken down by layer (design / coating / photochromic) so you know what you're actually paying for.

2. Check the fit against your actual prescription

Progressive lenses need an accurate pupil center measurement and some adjustment time. Ask how long that adjustment is expected to take before you commit.

3. Ask exactly what the warranty covers

M-LonG offers a 1-for-1 exchange within 7 days if the lens has a product defect.

4. Compare the price with and without each layer

Ask staff how much the price drops if you skip the photochromic layer, and whether you'd notice the difference day to day. It's the fastest way to find out which layer actually matters to you.

5. Don't pick the top tier just because it "feels safer"

A basic lens matched to your actual needs beats a premium lens used for the wrong purpose.

Keeping premium lenses lasting as long as the price suggests

Premium lenses hold up better than standard ones, but they're not indestructible. A few habits help:

  1. Clean with a microfiber cloth. Avoid clothing or tissue paper, which can scratch the coating.
  2. Rinse with clean water before wiping dry. Dust sitting on the lens is the most common cause of scratches when wiped dry directly.
  3. Store in a case when not wearing them. Avoid leaving glasses face-down on a table.
  4. Don't leave them in a hot car for long. High heat can affect the coating and the frame's plastic parts.
  5. Bring them in for periodic checks. M-LonG offers free adjustment and screw-tightening checks even if you're not buying anything.

Common questions

Do I really need premium lenses?

Not everyone needs all three upgrade layers. If you only look at a screen for a couple of hours a day, the blue-light filter alone may be enough. Our team recommends based on your actual daily eye use, not a default top-tier package.

Can photochromic lenses replace sunglasses?

In normal sun conditions, yes, they can replace carrying a separate pair of sunglasses. In very strong glare, for example driving or at the beach, dedicated polarized sunglasses are still more effective. That polarized line is currently only available at Manh Long Optical without prescription power.

Do progressive lenses need an adjustment period?

Yes. Progressive lenses combine multiple viewing zones in a single lens surface, so some people need a few days to adjust to looking through each zone correctly. This is why accurate pupil center measurement matters more for progressives than for single-vision lenses.

Does blue-light filtering really reduce eye strain?

Anti-reflective coating reduces glare from screens, which is what actually makes long screen sessions more comfortable. On eye strain specifically, current evidence (a 2023 Cochrane review, the American Academy of Ophthalmology) hasn't shown a clear effect from blue-light filtering alone.
